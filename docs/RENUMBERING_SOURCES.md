# Published sources on NYC and Brooklyn renumberings, 1786-1925

Date: 2026-11-14

Web research only. No ordinance text and no Common Council or Board of Aldermen proceedings could be reached. The one primary document read is the preface and listings of Lain's Brooklyn Directory for 1887 (Internet Archive item 1887BPL), which bears directly on the Eastern District. Everything else is a secondary or blog source. The work was done in two passes (the first stopped when the search tool hit its usage limit); the second pass filled gaps, and the "Not found" section says what remains.

Labels used for how a source was seen:
- OPENED: the page was fetched and read (through a summarizing fetch tool, so quotes are as that tool returned them; check wording against the page before citing in print).
- SNIPPET: seen only in a search-result summary. Treat as unverified until opened.

Confidence labels: confirmed by a primary source / stated by a secondary source / hearsay. Only the Lain's 1887 preface (section 2) reaches "primary", and only for the fact that Eastern District streets were renamed but not renumbered by June 1886; no ordinance is confirmed for any event.

## 1. Brooklyn, Western District (about 1870-72)

What is published:

- Jeremy Lechtzin's New York Times piece (Times Insider / reporting, around 29 January 2021). A scraped blog copy was OPENED at https://solokol.blogspot.com/2021/01/an-old-brooklyn-story-gets-fresh.html. It says "nearly every street address in Brooklyn was changed" in 1870, that fractional addresses (the "A" and "1/2" numbers) were "devised in a great reshuffling in 1870", and that the common belief is the change happened "around 1871 or '72", which the author calls unsatisfying. The copy names no ordinance, no ordering body and no rule. I did not reach the NYT original; the exact title is uncertain (one snippet gives "An Old Brooklyn Story Gets Fresh Reporting", the student site below cites "Brooklyn's Big Street Address Mess").
- liuj012.github.io/wherenumbersrest (OPENED, https://liuj012.github.io/wherenumbersrest). A student web project citing Lechtzin. It says: "In 1869, the Renumbering Committee proposed renumbering each block in ascending order, removing duplicate and fractional numbers, and reserving numbers for empty lots," and listed 75 streets to be renamed. The plan "was never fully effective"; fractional numbers returned. No ordinance number given. This is the only source found that names a body (a "Renumbering Committee") and a year (1869). It is a derived source: confirm in the Brooklyn Common Council proceedings of 1869-70.
- Brooklyn Public Library blog "152 Henry Street" (OPENED, https://www.bklynlibrary.org/blog/2010/11/23/152-henry-street): "Street addresses in Brooklyn Heights changed in 1870." An 1864 ad gives the house as 130 Henry; the author found the old number in the library's historical atlases.
- Center for Brooklyn History / BPL house-and-building research guide (OPENED, https://bklynlibrary.org/cbh/collections/research-guides/house-and-building): "The atlases are also helpful in determining addresses, many of which changed circa 1871-72." No conversion table. Contact for help: cbhreference@bklynlibrary.org.
- Novelty Theater, "Historic Street Names in North Brooklyn (and Beyond)" (OPENED, https://noveltytheater.net/node/1171): the 1870 "great Brooklyn street renumbering"; before 1871 Williamsburg odd numbers were on the south side and even on the north, and from 1870 they were flipped to match most of Brooklyn and NYC. Cites Lain's Brooklyn City and Business Directory (year ending May 1, 1871) for 1870 renamings.
- Brownstoner on 24 Middagh Street (SNIPPET only): renumbered around 1871; an 1870 ad gives 42 Middagh.
- Union Street Project blog (OPENED, https://lostnewyorkcity.blogspot.com/2011/04/union-street-project-149-union-street.html): the blogger compared an 1855 map and a 1927 map and found 149 Union St was 114 Union St in 1855 (a block-wide shift, no formula). A commenter says Carroll Gardens Patch put the change "between 1863 and the 1870s" (hearsay, Patch article not found).
- Brooklynology (BPL) post on the January 1871 volume of Board of Aldermen proceedings (SNIPPET only, https://www.bklynlibrary.org/blogs/brooklynology?page=27): says the Brooklyn Historical Society holds a largely complete run of the Proceedings of the Board of Aldermen of the City of Brooklyn. That is where the ordinance should be.

Rule used: partly known. The 1869 plan (per the student site) wanted ascending numbers per block, no duplicates or fractions, numbers reserved for vacant lots. Parity rule: odd on one side, even on the other, flipped in Williamsburg. The per-foot spacing, the starting end and the effective date are NOT found.

Relation to the project's detection (1871 vs 1875 Lain's): published sources say 1870 (some 1871-72). The 1871 volume is "1870-71", compiled in 1870 (OPENED, see section 2), and its front matter says nothing about new numbers. A change in 1870 itself would put new numbers in the 1871 volume, against the project's detection; the BPL "circa 1871-72" and the Lechtzin "1871 or '72" fit a change after that volume. The sources do not settle whether "1870" means the plan or the effect. The project's own data (residents who stayed put, change between 1871 and 1875) is better evidence of when numbers took effect than the blog dates; the likely reading is that the 1869-70 plan took effect in 1871-72. Cheap check: sample Henry St and Middagh St in the 1870, 1871, 1872 and 1873 volumes (152 Henry = 130 Henry; 24 Middagh = 42 Middagh).

## 2. Williamsburg / Eastern District (project: between 1887 and 1889)

From the Novelty Theater page (OPENED):

- 1802 grid: north/south streets numbered, Grand St the zero line.
- 1855: Armbruster (1912) says "the numbered streets of Bushwick and Greenpoint were altered at the time of the consolidation with Brooklyn in 1855."
- 1870: parity flip (see above).
- 1885: a proposed renaming of the numbered streets vetoed, "only three months before election day" (Brooklyn Union, August 27, 1885, per the site).
- 1886: the numbered streets were renamed, mostly after the connecting streets on the Brooklyn side of Division Avenue (Brooklyn Daily Eagle, April 13, 1886, per the site). Under the new scheme Bedford Avenue numbers started at Manhattan Avenue and rose southward, while the former Fourth Street numbers ran north from Division Avenue. This matches the project's finding that 4th St E.D. became Bedford Av and that some streets are numbered from the other end.
- 1889-1897: "many current numbering schemes, such as Grand Street's, date from this period", suggesting a possible third renumbering. The page itself hedges.
- A Forgotten New York comment (SNIPPET only) says Fifth Street was renamed Driggs in 1886 and its numbering reversed, and that Armbruster gives 1889 for the street-name changes. Unopened; hearsay.

Primary evidence, OPENED (second pass): Lain's Brooklyn Directory for the year ending May 1, 1887 (https://archive.org/details/1887BPL; text at https://archive.org/download/1887BPL/1887BPL_djvu.txt). Its preface is dated "Brooklyn, June, 1886" and says of "the twelve numerical streets, which were renamed, but not renumbered" that the publishers took extra care to make locations on them intelligible, and: "It is only regretted that the new numbers were not in time for this edition." So by June 1886 the Eastern District's twelve numbered streets had new names but old numbers, and new numbers were expected after that. The listings use "old No." (e.g. "h old No. 237 Bushwick av", "old No. 148 Kent av", "old No. 635 Bedford av") and "now" renames (e.g. "64 4th E.D. now Bedford av", "81 7th E.D. now Havemoyer", "24 10th E.D. now Keap", "92 1st E.D. now Kent av", "205 2d E.D. now Wythe av"). The preface does not name an ordinance, board or date, and does not use the words "Eastern District".

Reading: this agrees with the project's detection. The 1886 renaming (BDE April 13, 1886, per Novelty Theater) came first; the numbers changed later, after the 1887 directory and before 1889. So the "1886" in the blog sources is the renaming, not the number change, and there is no real contradiction. A caveat: the 1889 directory's preface (dated June 1888, OPENED, first 100k characters only) says nothing about numbers, so no published statement was found fixing the month the new numbers took effect. Still unknown: the ordinance, the rule (which end, per-foot spacing), and the effective date. Look in Brooklyn Board of Aldermen proceedings 1886-1888 and the Brooklyn Daily Eagle 1886-1888. Also opened: the Lain's 1871 volume (title "The Brooklyn City and Business Directory", "1870-71"; first 100k characters only): its notice and preface say nothing about numbers or renumbering. It was compiled in 1870 (copyright notice), which is consistent with it printing the old Western District numbers.

## 4. Manhattan avenues (Third, Eighth, Ninth, First, Second, Avenue A), 1865-1876

Not found as an ordinance. What was found:

- Tribeca Citizen (OPENED, https://tribecacitizen.com/2017/10/10/nosy-neighbor-why-are-tribecas-street-numbers-messed-up/), a blog piece quoting Hoffmann 1937, lists: 1793/94 first law regulating uniform street numbers (odd and even on opposite sides); 1794 Reade St numbers first assigned and Wall St renumbered; 1811 grid; 1838 law dividing Manhattan into East and West Sides; 1845 Wall St renumbered again; 1861 law addressing numbering problems from construction and demolition north of 9th Street. The post says these dates are not all from Hoffmann. This is blog-level, no citations to the laws themselves.
- The "1861 law ... north of 9th Street" is the only legislative hook in the 1865-76 neighbourhood. Check the Laws of New York 1861 and the Manhattan Board of Aldermen records.

Rose-Redwood's account (SNIPPET only) of Manhattan: a 1940 debate about renumbering avenue addresses, covered by the New York Times in January 1940.

Second pass additions:

- 6sqft (OPENED, https://www.6sqft.com/?p=85722): "in 1838 the city began using Fifth Avenue as a dividing line"; decimal numbering for cross streets began in 1861, 100 numbers per block between two avenues; the 1940 avenue renumbering proposal came from the postmaster and was dropped after the Fifth Avenue Association objected.
- Wikipedia "Manhattan address algorithm" (OPENED): avenue lots "originally 20 feet wide" (citing Ballon, ed., *The Greatest Grid*, 2013, p. 87); York Avenue continues Avenue A's numbers and East End Avenue continues Avenue B's; "1 Park Avenue" address later changed (citing Christopher Gray, "History Lessons by the Numbers," NYT, 7 Nov 2008). It gives no renumbering dates for the 1860s-70s.
- Board of Aldermen renumbering resolutions (SNIPPET only; the a860-gpp.nyc.gov City Record PDFs returned HTTP 403): April 1895, West Broadway renumbered with numbers to commence at Vesey Street; 1897, Vanderbilt (Park) Avenue in the 23rd and 24th Wards, numbers running from the Harlem River up, "the old numbers for Vanderbilt avenue". These show renumbering by Aldermen resolution published in the City Record, and that the City Record is the place to look; they are outside the 1865-76 window. Ask the Municipal Archives / NYPL for the City Record index.
- Rule hint: since avenue lots were 20 feet wide, a Manhattan avenue numbering rule of about one number per 20-25 feet is plausible, but no source examined states one for the avenues.

## 6. Sixth Avenue, 1920s

Found, secondary. Wikipedia "Sixth Avenue" (OPENED, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_Avenue): "After the renumbering of the street's properties in 1929, the Sixth Avenue extension was opened to traffic in 1930" (its citation: Christiane Bird, *A Block in Time*, Bloomsbury 2022, p. 256). Search summaries (SNIPPET only: Forgotten New York "Deep Sixth", "The End of Macdougal", and others) say the extension south of Carmine Street began in 1926 (Holland Tunnel approach, widening Minetta Lane to Canal Street), that all addresses were renumbered to fit it, numbering from the new southern end at Church and Franklin Streets, and that Seventh Avenue South was kept as a separate street with its own numbers. Wikipedia also records an unrelated 1887 rename of the Harlem part of Sixth Avenue to Lenox Avenue (NYT, 5 Oct 1887, p. 4). So the "1920s" claim is supported: 1929, tied to the extension, not a citywide avenue plan. The 1940 plan to renumber all avenues (6sqft, OPENED) was dropped and is a different event. No ordinance found; the 1929 rule is described only as "renumbered from the new southern end".

## 7. Other renumberings, 1786-1925

Found:

- Manhattan, from Hoffmann via Tribeca Citizen: Cedar St extended and renumbered 1829; Canal St 1837; Provost St annexed to Franklin St 1833, with Franklin numbers reversed (this explains why Franklin east of West Broadway runs the opposite way). OPENED (Tribeca Citizen).
- Manhattan 1793/94 first numbering law, 1794 Reade/Wall, 1845 Wall (same source).
- Brooklyn: 1855 changes to Bushwick and Greenpoint numbered streets at consolidation (Novelty Theater citing Armbruster, OPENED).
- Brooklyn 1873: "Chapter 863, Laws of 1873" is a ward-wide TAX BLOCK numbering system (NYC Municipal Archives collection guide, SNIPPET only, https://a860-collectionguides.nyc.gov/repositories/2/accessions/1288). This is not house numbering; do not confuse it with a renumbering.
- Queens, 1915-1926: out of scope, but the rule is documented in Charles U. Powell, "Bringing Order Out of Chaos in Street Naming and House Numbering," The American City, February 1928 (OPENED, republished at https://junipercivic.com/history/bringing-order-out-of-chaos-in-street-naming-and-house-numbering): numbers have two parts, the nearest cross street and the distance in 20-foot lot intervals, odd on north sides of avenues and east sides of streets. Street names change only by Board of Aldermen resolution, between 1 December and 1 May. This is a useful analogue for the rule style, not evidence for Brooklyn.
- Legal point (SNIPPET only): *Bacon v. Miller*, 160 N.E. 381 (N.Y.) holds there is no vested right in a street name or house number, so cities can renumber freely (cited in an APA PAS report, https://www.planning.org/pas/reports/report13.htm).

- An NYPL digitized 1893 "Index to streets and house numbers" for Manhattan (SNIPPET only, https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/a1ea284f-c7f7-bf9d-e040-e00a18061bc3): a street/house-number index to an atlas, not an old-to-new table.
- A City Record page reproducing "an ordinance on the numbering and renumbering of houses in the streets of this city" (SNIPPET only, a860-gpp.nyc.gov, probably 1880s-90s; 403 on fetch). If it is the Manhattan ordinance it would state the standing rule; worth getting by hand.

## Literature

Hoffmann 1937:

- Henry B. Hoffmann, "Changed House Numbers and Lost Street Names in New York of the Early Nineteenth Century and Later," *New-York Historical Society Quarterly Bulletin* 21, no. 3 (1937), 67-92. Citation seen in a bibliography, the NYHS index (1917-1980) and Rose-Redwood's Urban History article, all via SNIPPET. The title/author/pages are consistent across them.
- Content (OPENED, only as quoted by Tribeca Citizen): "There are four basic types of house-number change which have taken place in New York City": (1) a simple shift in numbers; (2) extension of a street at the end where numbers formerly began (Cedar 1829, Canal 1837); (3) merging of streets that join end to end; (4) the reverse, one street becoming two or more. "Places" and "Squares" sometimes formed separate address systems. His one Tribeca example is Franklin/Provost 1833. This is a pre-1850 Manhattan study; no evidence it covers Brooklyn.
- Where to read it: not found online. archive.org holds NYHS Quarterly Bulletin volumes 1-8 (1917-1925) only, per a search of identifiers newyorkhistorica00newy, newyorkhistorica6819newy and newyorkhistoric00socigoog (OPENED search results). Volume 21 (1937) was not found there. Try JSTOR, HathiTrust and the New-York Historical Society library; I did not verify any of them.

Rose-Redwood:

- "Indexing the great ledger of the community: urban house numbering, city directories and the production of spatial legibility," *Journal of Historical Geography* 34 (2008), 286-310. Citation seen on the ScienceDirect listing (SNIPPET only; the page itself returned 403). The partial abstract reported by the search tool: a critical spatial history of street and house numbering in the gridded cities of the United States since the eighteenth century; few studies have explored the historical geography of house numbering; city-directory publishers presented themselves as offering a public good, hence "great ledger of the community". Full text not read; the search tool also said the 2012 Urban History article cites it (pp. 300-1) for a point about New York house numbering (SNIPPET only). Nothing verified about specific NYC renumbering dates in it.
- "'A regular state of beautiful confusion': governing by numbers and the contradictions of calculable space in New York City," *Urban History* 39, no. 4 (November 2012), 624-638, DOI 10.1017/S0963926812000399. Abstract OPENED at https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/urban-history/article/abs/regular-state-of-beautiful-confusion-governing-by-numbers-and-the-contradictions-of-calculable-space-in-new-york-city/50097499D994E4B19C00163757F9E0F3: house numbering has been "a political technology of spatial calculation" since the eighteenth century; the article argues it was a key way of making Manhattan calculable and that these systems produced contradictions that led to several rounds of spatial rationalization in the 1700s and 1800s. Full text is paywalled; reference list not seen. I could not verify what it says about specific dates; the search tool also reported it cites Hoffmann (1937) and discusses the January 1940 renumbering debate (SNIPPET only).
- "With numbers in place: security, territory, and the production of calculable space," *Annals of the AAG* 102, no. 2 (2012), 295-319 (SNIPPET only; this is probably the "2012 paper" your brief means, or it is the Urban History one; the title in the brief did not match either exactly). Content not read.
- Rose-Redwood and Kadonaga, "'The Corner of Avenue A and Twenty-Third Street': Geographies of Street Numbering in the United States," *Professional Geographer* 68, no. 1 (2016), 39-52 (SNIPPET only). Content not read.
- Rose-Redwood's own earlier statement (via the Urban History search result, SNIPPET): very few secondary sources document the history of house numbering in New York City.

For every edition held in more than one scan, the pairs pass picks one copy to run: the whole alphabet first, then the most lines per page. Each excerpt shows the same printed lines in every copy: the scan's crop, then the OCR lines the model would read. Two OCRs agreeing usually means both are right, but copies set in the same typeface can share a misreading (the Franks long s, read as f in three copies at once). Where they differ, the crop shows which one is right. Entry-shaped lines is the ad-page inventory's measure (a residence marker with a number, a name then an address, and so on), counted over every listing page.

Franks 1786, New York's first directory: four reprints

4 copies; 2,718 scoped lines in the copies not chosen. Title-page years read: 1786.

My read. None of the four is the 1786 printing. The pick and fran_0 are two scans of the 1889 facsimile reprint. newyorkbrooklynd00durs (1876) resets the pages one for one. All three keep the long s, which the OCR reads as f (ftreet, Jofeph). durs_0, whose title page gives a reprint year of 1851 (IA dates it 1874), reset the type with a modern s. Its OCR reads 57% of the gold lines exactly, against 5% or less for the other three, and 98% of them at ≥0.8. The cost is provenance: records would cite an 1851 reprint's pages instead of a facsimile of the original. Either way it is about 905 lines. On the random pages, the three long-s copies agree with each other line for line, misreadings included (Water-ftreet), so their green marks there show agreement, not accuracy. The gold page is the fair test.
